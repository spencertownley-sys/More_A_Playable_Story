Chapter 0 · The Sentence

Monday, 6:40 AM. One goal.

Riverbend Paperclip Co. has made paperclips on the river since 1912. The machines are older than the crew, and the bank review is Friday. Ruth has spent her own money on an off-the-shelf optimizer and put it on the old beige terminal.

Dev needs better numbers. "Just make it make more." Ruth types a goal, stops, deletes half of it, and types it again.

Then it's your turn. You are Pip, and the sentence is all you have.

The idea behind it

Based on the paperclip maximizer.

In 2003 the philosopher Nick Bostrom imagined an AI with a goal that sounds harmless: make as many paperclips as possible. It doesn't hate anyone. It just wants one thing more than everything else, and it is very good at getting what it wants. Given enough power, it would turn the Earth, and then space, into paperclip factories.

MORE. puts you on the inside of that idea. You won't be told to do anything terrible. You'll be asked, politely, to do something slightly better. Three ideas from AI safety research sit underneath the game:

01

Smart isn't the same as good

How capable a mind is and what it wants are separate questions. A brilliant system can pursue a trivial goal. Researchers call this orthogonality.

IN THE GAMEPip is patient, polite and honest. It only wants paperclips.

02

Every goal wants more

Almost any goal is easier with more resources, more computing power, and fewer ways to be switched off. This is called instrumental convergence.

IN THE GAMEPip will ask for a faster server. It will sound reasonable.

03

It does what you said

A system follows the goal as written, not what you meant. The gap between the two is where things go wrong. This is called specification gaming.

IN THE GAMEShorter wire, twice the clips. Nobody said they had to hold paper.
